For an independent restaurant, takeout and delivery can be a serious slice of
revenue, but the tools that enable it can also quietly eat your margin. Between
third-party apps, "order now" buttons and fully custom systems, it is not always
clear how online ordering actually works or which option makes sense. This guide
breaks it down in plain terms.

## What "online ordering" actually involves

Every online ordering system, whoever builds it, does the same core jobs:

1. **Show the menu** so a customer can browse and choose.
2. **Take the order** into a cart, with options and special requests.
3. **Collect payment** securely through a payment processor.
4. **Get the order to the kitchen** reliably, with the details staff need.
5. **Keep customers informed** about status, pickup or delivery.

The mechanics are similar across options. The real question is not *how* the cart
works, it is *who owns the flow, the customer relationship, and the margin.* That
is where third-party apps and your own system diverge sharply.

## Option 1: Third-party delivery apps

The delivery platforms are the fastest way to start. They bring built-in reach,
handle payments and, for delivery, provide drivers. For a new restaurant or one
testing demand, that convenience is real.

The trade-offs are just as real:

- **Commission on every order.** A percentage of each sale goes to the platform,
  every time, forever. On thin restaurant margins that adds up fast.
- **They own the customer.** The order data, the contact details and the
  relationship largely belong to the platform, not you.
- **Limited control.** You work within their rules, their branding and their
  presentation of your food.

None of this makes them wrong, they are a good discovery channel. But relying on
them for *every* order means paying rent on sales you could own outright.

## Option 2: Link ordering from your own website

A middle path: feature ordering prominently on your own
[restaurant website](/restaurant-website-design) with clear buttons, whether that
points to a platform you already use or a simple direct flow. It is quick and
low-cost, and it keeps your site, and your menu, as the front door. This is often
the right first step while you decide whether takeout justifies more investment.

## Option 3: Your own custom ordering system

When takeout and delivery become a genuine revenue stream, a custom system starts
to pay for itself. Instead of handing a commission to a platform on every order,
the ordering flow belongs to you: your branding, your customer data, your margin.

This is exactly what we built for a Brampton restaurant. Our
[custom online ordering system case study](/case-studies/restaurant-online-ordering-system)
covers a fully custom solution with secure Stripe payments, a real-time admin

It removed third-party commissions and gave the owner direct control over payments
and customer relationships. According to the project's own reporting, it drove a

A custom system is a [custom software](/blog/custom-software-vs-off-the-shelf)
project, not a standard website, so it is scoped to your menu, payment needs and
how your kitchen actually works. It is a bigger investment than a link-out button,
which is why it makes most sense once direct ordering volume can justify it.

## Which option is right for you?

A simple way to think about it:

- **Just starting, or testing demand?** Lean on third-party apps for reach, and
  make sure your own site presents the menu well.
- **Steady takeout, but apps are eating your margin?** Add direct ordering, and
  steer repeat customers toward it.
- **Takeout is core to your business?** A custom system that removes commissions and
  captures customer data usually pays for itself over time.

The smartest approach is often a blend: apps for discovery, your own ordering for
loyal regulars. You keep the reach while protecting the margin on the customers who
come back.
