"Do I need a website or a web application?" sounds like a technical question, but
it is really a business one. Pick the wrong side and you either overspend on
machinery you do not need, or you build a pretty brochure when what your business
actually needs is a tool. This guide explains the difference in plain English and
gives you a simple way to tell which one fits your situation.

## The core difference: showing vs doing

The clearest way to think about it:

- A **website** mostly *shows* information. Your homepage, services, about page
  and contact form look much the same for every visitor and change only when you
  update them. Its job is to make you credible, be found in search, and turn
  visitors into enquiries.
- A **web application** *does* something with data. It reacts to who is using it
  and what they do: logging in, searching, filtering, booking, ordering, or
  seeing a dashboard built from live information. Its job is to run a process, not
  just present a page.

Most small businesses need an excellent website first. A smaller number also need
an application behind it, and some need both stitched together. The trick is
knowing which problem you are actually solving.

## At a glance

| | Website | Web application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary job** | Show information, build trust, get enquiries | Run a process or manage data |
| **Content per visitor** | Largely the same for everyone | Changes based on the user and their actions |
| **Typical features** | Pages, images, forms, blog, SEO | Logins, dashboards, search, bookings, payments |
| **Who updates it** | You, occasionally | Users, continuously, as they use it |
| **Starts from (Mintek)** | CAD $1,500 | CAD $5,000 |
| **Best when** | You need to be found and look credible | Your product *is* the software |

The rest of this guide helps you place your own situation in that table.

## Signs you need a website

A marketing website is the right call, and usually all you need, when:

- **Your main goal is to be found and look credible.** Local search, a clear
  services page and a contact form that reliably delivers enquiries cover most of
  what a service business needs.
- **Your content is fairly stable.** Services, about, pricing and contact details
  do not change hour to hour.
- **You want leads, not logins.** You are trying to start conversations, not run a
  live system your customers operate themselves.

If that sounds like you, resist the urge to overbuild. A fast, well-structured
site is often the highest-return investment a small business can make. Our

[how much a business website costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-business-website-cost-in-the-gta)
breaks down the ranges.

Even content that changes often does not always require an application. We have

clean, structured experience.

## Signs you need a web application

A web application starts to make sense when a plain site can no longer carry the
load:

- **Users need to log in.** The moment different people should see different
  things, accounts and permissions push you into application territory.
- **The content is different for every user.** A dashboard, an order history, a
  personalised list, anything driven by *who is looking* and *what they have
  done*, is application behaviour.
- **You are running a process, not publishing a page.** Bookings, online ordering,
  search-and-filter over lots of records, or a two-sided marketplace are all real
  systems with their own logic and edge cases.
- **You need live, reliable data.** When the value is in up-to-date information
  and the actions people take on it, you have outgrown a static site.

map-based discovery, listing management and structured data designed to scale.
That is not something a page builder can fake.

## The grey area (and how to handle it)

Plenty of projects live in between, and that is fine. An online store, a booking
page, or a "website" with one interactive tool all mix showing and doing. The
useful question is not "which label applies?" but **"how much does this thing need
to *do*?"**

The more it displays, the more it is a website. The more it reacts to users and
manages data, the more it is an application. When a marketing site starts sprouting
logins, dashboards or real transactions, you are quietly scoping an application,
and it is better to plan for that than to bolt it onto the wrong foundation.

This is the same "almost fits" trap we describe in our guide on
[custom software vs off-the-shelf](/blog/custom-software-vs-off-the-shelf): forcing
application-shaped needs into website-shaped tools creates a slow tax of
workarounds. Naming the project honestly up front saves money later.

## Don't overbuild: start with the goal

The most expensive mistake is building an application when a website would do, or
a page builder when you needed a real system. Both waste money in opposite
directions. So start with the outcome, not the technology:

1. **What is the single most important thing this needs to achieve?** More
   enquiries? Let customers book? Manage listings?
2. **Does reaching that goal require users to *act on data*,** or just to *read and
   contact you*?
3. **What is the smallest version that delivers it?** You can almost always launch
   focused and expand later.

If the honest answer is "we mostly need to be found and look credible," you need a
website. If it is "our customers or team need to *do* something here," you are
looking at a web application. If you are genuinely unsure, that is exactly what a
short discovery conversation is for.
