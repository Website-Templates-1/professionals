"We need an app" is one of the most common things a business owner says — and one
of the most expensive to get wrong. Hidden inside it is a real decision: do you
build a native app users install from the app stores, or a web app they open in a
browser? Picking the wrong one can mean spending far more than you needed to, or
building something users cannot easily reach. Here is how to think it through.

## The core difference

- A **native app** is installed from the App Store or Google Play and runs
  directly on the phone. It can make full use of device features, work offline
  more easily, and sit as an icon on the home screen.
- A **web app** runs in the browser at a web address. There is nothing to
  install, it works across devices, and updates appear instantly for everyone.

Neither is "better" in the abstract. The right choice depends entirely on what
your product needs to do and how users will reach it.

## At a glance

| Factor | Native app | Web app |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **How users get it** | Install from an app store | Open a URL, nothing to install |
| **Reach** | iOS and Android (per platform) | Any device with a browser |
| **Device features / offline** | Fullest access | More limited |
| **Updates** | Via store review | Instant for all users |
| **Typical cost to start** | Higher | Lower |
| **Best when** | You need installed presence, push, or deep device features | You need fast reach and easy iteration |

## When a web app is the right first step

For many first mobile products, a web app is the smarter starting point. It makes
sense when:

- **Reach and speed matter most.** No install friction and no app-store review
  means you get in front of users, and iterate, quickly.
- **You are validating demand.** If you are still proving people want the thing,
  a web app is a cheaper, faster way to find out.
- **It works across devices.** One build serves phones, tablets and desktops.

A lot of things people call "an app" are really a
[web application](/web-application-development) — dashboards, booking tools,
internal tools, marketplaces. If your product mostly needs to *do* something with
data and be easy to reach, the browser is often the right home.

## When native is worth it

A native app earns its extra cost when:

- **You need deep device features** — reliable push notifications, camera,
  sensors, or strong offline use.
- **Users expect to find you in the stores**, and an installed icon matters to
  the experience.
- **Performance and feel are central** to a smooth, frequently used product.

When native is the right call, building separately for iOS and Android is slow

## Start with the outcome, not the format

The most expensive mistake is choosing the format first and forcing your idea
into it. Instead, start with your goal and let it point to the answer:

1. **What is the core thing users need to do?**
2. **Does that require deep device features or offline use** — or mostly data and
   a good interface?
3. **How will users realistically discover and reach it?**
4. **What is the smallest version that proves the idea?**

If the honest answers lean toward reach, speed and data, a web app is likely your
best first step. If they lean toward device features and installed presence, plan
for native.

## You can start focused and grow

These are not mutually exclusive forever. Plenty of products launch as a web app
to validate demand, then invest in a native app once real usage justifies it.
That staged approach keeps early cost and risk down — the same logic behind
[building an MVP first](/blog/mvp-first-why-start-small). Starting focused does not
close doors; it keeps them open until you have evidence about which one to walk
through.
