On **87** successfully fetched homepages of independently operated Brampton businesses, homepage testimonial or review blocks matching the study’s text patterns appeared on **17**. The study write-up calls that share **19.1%**.

Seventeen out of 87 is **19.5%** when rounded to one decimal, and that is the figure on the published study page. The **19.1%** label is the same **17** matches on an earlier fetch count of **89**. The count did not move. This article is about those 17 pattern matches, taken on **11 September 2026**, from the homepages drawn for [The State of Brampton Business Websites: 2026](/research/brampton-business-websites-2026).

**17 is not “17 Brampton businesses have reviews,” and it is not “the rest have none.”** It is the number of fetched homepages whose visible text contained one of a short list of words. The detector did not open Google, did not count stars, and did not decide whether a sentence was true.

## What was measured

The check ran on the homepage text after the HTML was fetched. It looked for any one of these:

- the word “testimonial”
- the phrase “google review”
- “rated” followed by a digit
- the word “star” or “stars”
- “what our clients say,” and the same shape with “my,” “customers,” or “patients”

One match was enough. The homepage was then marked present. There was no second pass to confirm a quote, a name, a date, or a link to the source.

That is a wording check. It is not a count of review cards. It is not a count of Google Business Profile ratings. It is not review volume, and it is not review quality. A page that says “What our patients say” and then shows three real sentences counts the same as a page that merely contains the word “stars.” A page full of genuine quotes that never uses those words counts as absent.

The word “star” is the wide part of the net. It can match a rating. It can also match copy that is not a testimonial at all. The study’s own limits already say this kind of detection can false-positive and false-negative. **17** is the size of the net’s catch. It is not an audit of proof.

Nothing in the inspect submitted a review, opened a profile, or compared the homepage sentence to the review a customer actually left. Named businesses elsewhere in the study are measurements of other signals. This article does not add a verdict on any of them.

## Homepage proof and Google proof are different places

A Google review lives on Google. The profile collects it, shows the rating next to the listing, and keeps the reviewer’s name on Google’s page. Someone searching “dentist Brampton” can see that rating in the map results without ever opening the website.

Homepage proof is whatever the site itself says. A quote in the first screen. A row of stars in the design. A link that says the reviews are on Google. A paragraph that claims “rated 5 stars” with no trail back to a source. Those are page decisions. They can agree with the profile. They can also drift away from it: an old quote, a round number, a widget that shows a different total than the listing.

The 2026 inspect never compared the two. Places supplied a rating and a review count when the sample was built, and within each search the eligible businesses were ordered by that Google review count before the cap was applied. The HTML check did not publish those Places numbers, and it did not score the homepage against them. A business can sit near the top of a Places search because people have reviewed it, and still have a homepage with none of the trigger phrases. A homepage can say “testimonials” and still have a thin profile. Both can be true. This file only recorded the phrase.

That is why proof on the site and proof on Google answer different questions. The profile is where Google’s reviewers spoke. The homepage is where the business chooses what a visitor sees after they click through. [Local SEO for GTA service businesses](/local-seo-gta) is the page for how the profile, the name and address, and the site fit together. This article does not turn the 17 matches into a ranking result.

## Copying a review without rights or context

A review is someone else’s sentence about a specific visit. Pasting it onto a homepage is a reuse. The study’s own notes say to show a small set of reviews when they exist and when the rights allow. The inspect did not check rights. A pattern match does not mean the business had permission, and a missing pattern does not mean they were careful.

Context is the other half. A sentence pulled out of a longer review can drop the service, the date, and the complaint that came after the compliment. A first name with no visit attached can be anyone. A block of five glowing lines with no dates can be five years old. The visitor cannot tell. The detector could not tell either. It only saw that a trigger word was on the page.

The safer homepage move, when the reviews already live on Google, is to point at that profile rather than to rebuild it in your own words. A link keeps the rating, the count, and the full text where the reviewer left them. A short quote is reasonable when you have the right to use it, the person’s name, and enough context that the sentence still means what they wrote. A wall of unattributed praise is a design choice. It is not what this study measured, and it is not automatically proof.

## Fake reviews, fake ratings, and invented schema

Three different fakes get lumped together. They are different acts, and none of them is in the 17.

**A fake review** is a review that did not come from a customer. Someone writes it, or pays for it, and it appears on Google or on the site as if a client said it. The study did not investigate that. A phrase match cannot see authorship.

**A fake rating** is a number the page asserts. “Rated 5.0” in a badge, with no reviews behind it, or with a count that is not the profile’s count. The detector would often catch the word “rated” or “stars.” Catching the word is not the same as checking the number. The inspect never compared a homepage rating to Places.

**Schema that invents an AggregateRating** is the machine-readable version of the same problem. JSON-LD can state a `ratingValue` and a `reviewCount` that the visible page does not earn. That markup is a claim. If the numbers were never collected, the claim is false whether or not a person notices the script. The 2026 homepage pass did not audit AggregateRating blocks. The schema count in the study is about LocalBusiness-style types, which is a different article: [LocalBusiness Schema: How Many Brampton Websites Actually Use It?](/blog/localbusiness-schema-brampton-websites). Marking up a rating of your own business, on your own site, is also the pattern Google treats as self-serving. It does not produce star rich results for that business. Inventing the block does not fix that, and it is not a ranking tactic.

The honest version is smaller. Real reviews stay attributed. A rating on the page matches a source a visitor can open. The structured data, if you publish any, repeats those facts and does not improve on them. This study did not measure whether anyone did that well. It measured whether a few words appeared.

## Why 19.1% is not “Brampton businesses have no reviews”

Read the percentage as a label on a text search.

**70 of the 87** fetched homepages did not contain the trigger phrases. That is the complement of 17. It means the wording was absent from that snapshot. It does not mean the business has no Google reviews, no binder of thank-you notes, and no reputation in the neighbourhood. Reviews on the contact page, inside an iframe the fetch did not read as those words, or written as “clients tell us” without the listed phrase, all land in the 70. So does a homepage that simply never mentions reviews and lets the profile carry them.

The sample makes the misreading worse if you treat it as a census of reputation. These were businesses that appeared in Places for the chosen queries, already had a website, and were ordered by Google review count inside each search before the cap. The file is tilted toward listings people have already reviewed. Homepage silence in that file is evidence about the homepage, not evidence that Brampton has no reviews.

**19.1%** and **19.5%** are the same 17 homepages with a different denominator or a different rounding. Neither number is a quality score. A matched page can be a single stray “star.” An unmatched page can be a practice with a long Google history and a quiet homepage. The useful statement is the count, plus the instrument: 17 homepages matched a phrase list on one day.

## Prices were rare, and that is not a defect

On-page pricing language was rare. Phrases such as “our pricing,” “price list,” “starting at,” “from” plus a dollar amount, or “fee guide” appeared on **4 of 87** homepages. The study write-up calls that **4.5%**. Four out of 87 rounds to **4.6%** in the published stats. The share is small either way.

The study does not treat a missing price as a failure. Many professional services cannot put a single number on the homepage without pretending the work is a product on a shelf. A dentist, a lawyer, and an accountant often need to know the problem before they know the fee. A restaurant can publish a menu and still keep catering as a conversation. Absence of those five phrases is also a wording check: a page can show a number in a table, an image, or a sentence that says “projects start at” a figure without using “starting at,” and the detector would miss it.

Where a business does publish a real starting price, the page should show that price, not a teaser that the next screen retracts. Where the business quotes after a conversation, saying so is the accurate homepage. The 4-of-87 figure is not a target to hit.

## How we'd show proof without overclaiming

If we were handed a Brampton or GTA homepage tomorrow, we would not start by inserting the word “testimonials” until a detector passed. We would start from reviews that exist, and from what we are allowed to repeat.

**Send people to the profile when that is where the reviews are.** A clear link to the Google listing, with the business name, beats a pasted block the site does not have rights to host. The visitor can read the rating, the count, and the recent visits in one place. We would not retype a star average beside that link unless we have checked it against the profile.

**Use a small set on the page only with permission and context.** Name the person. Keep enough of the point that the quote is still about a real job: the service, the visit, the rough time. A handful is enough. The research notes already say a small set, when the reviews exist and the rights allow. We would rather show three sentences a client agreed to than a carousel of anonymous praise.

**Leave the slogan in the headline, and keep it out of the proof.** “Trusted in Brampton” is a position. It becomes proof only when a specific person said a specific thing. If we do not have that sentence, we do not invent one, and we do not draw a star row to decorate the hero.

**Do not publish a rating the business did not earn.** No badge with a number we chose. No AggregateRating block for a count we do not have. If structured data mentions reviews at all, it repeats a real quote and a real rating, and it does not ask Google for stars in return. This study did not find that testimonials move rankings. We would not add them for that reason.

**Say the price when it is a real published price, and skip the theatre when it is not.** A focused marketing site can start from a figure the business actually charges. A practice that scopes the work first can say the quote comes after a conversation. Neither choice is a defect in this file, and neither one requires a “price list” heading to satisfy a text search.

## What this study can and cannot tell us

**It can tell us** what one text search recorded on 87 public homepages on one day. A testimonial or review pattern matched on **17**. The write-up labels that **19.1%**; the published page, using 17 of 87, shows **19.5%**. Pricing phrases matched on **4 of 87**.

**It cannot tell us** how many Google reviews these businesses have, whether the quotes were real, whether anyone had the right to reprint them, or whether a testimonial changed a call or a ranking. There is no paired Mississauga sample. Six homepages never came back. The word “star” can count a page that has no reviews. A quiet homepage can belong to a business people review every week. The sample was ordered by Google review count, so it was never a portrait of businesses with empty reputations.

A homepage phrase does not, on this evidence, improve rankings. A missing phrase does not mean the business has no reviews.

## Conclusion

The 2026 Brampton homepage study did not find that the city is without reviews. It found a testimonial or review wording pattern on **17 of 87** fetched homepages. The report calls that **19.1%**. The same 17 on the final 87 round to **19.5%** on the study page. Both labels sit on a phrase list: “testimonial,” “google review,” “rated” and a digit, “star,” and “what our clients say.”

That list is not a Google Business Profile rating, not a review count, and not a judgment of quality. Proof on the homepage is the business choosing what to show. Proof on Google is what customers wrote on the profile. Copying one onto the other needs rights and context. A fake review, a made-up star average, and an AggregateRating that invents both are false claims. They are not in the 17, and they are not a ranking strategy. A missing price phrase, **4 of 87**, is not a defect.
