Marketplaces look simple from the outside — a site where two groups of people
find each other — but they are one of the harder things to build well. You are
not launching one product; you are launching two at once, for two different
audiences, and neither is useful without the other. Getting the planning right
before you build saves enormous cost later. Here is what to think through first.

## The core loop is everything

Before features, before design, answer one question: **what is the single
exchange that creates value on your platform?** In our
[Rent a Parking](/case-studies/parking-marketplace-platform) marketplace, it was
a driver finding and connecting with someone who has an unused parking space.
Everything else exists to make that one loop happen more often and more easily.

If you cannot describe your core loop in a sentence, you are not ready to build
yet. Every feature you consider should be judged by whether it strengthens that
loop.

## Plan for both sides — and the chicken-and-egg problem

A marketplace has to serve two audiences with different needs:

- **The supply side** (sellers, hosts, space owners) needs it to be easy to
  create and manage listings, and worth their time to show up.
- **The demand side** (buyers, renters, users) needs enough quality listings to
  find what they want, and trust that the platform works.

The catch is the classic chicken-and-egg problem: demand will not come without
supply, and supply will not bother without demand. Decide up front how you will
solve the cold start — often by focusing tightly on one category or one location
first, so a small amount of supply feels abundant to a small, targeted audience.

## The features that actually matter

Marketplaces share a recognisable core. Plan for these, and resist adding much
beyond them in a first version:

### Listings and listing management

The supply side needs to create, edit and manage listings, backed by structured
data behind the scenes. Good listing management keeps content fresh and
trustworthy — essential for a marketplace to grow.

### Search and filtering

The demand side needs to find the right listing fast. For many marketplaces,
especially location-based ones, that means robust search and filtering by
category, area or proximity.

### Maps and geographic discovery

If your marketplace is location-based, a map is often central rather than
decorative. We built Rent a Parking as a map-first experience with interactive
discovery and real-time filtering, because for parking, *where* is the whole
question. Fast, reliable map search deserves a solid technical foundation.

### Admin tooling

You need to oversee the marketplace — moderate listings, understand activity, and
keep quality high. This is easy to forget in early planning and painful to add
late.

## Decide on payments deliberately

One of the biggest early decisions is whether the first version handles payments.
It is tempting to assume you need them from day one, but many marketplaces prove
their core loop first — just connecting both sides — and add payments once the
model clearly works. Launching without payments can be a legitimate way to
validate demand cheaply. If your model genuinely requires payments up front, plan
for them; if not, they may belong in a later stage.

This is the same staged thinking we bring to every build: start with a focused
first version that proves the core, then expand. Our guide on
[why we recommend starting small](/blog/mvp-first-why-start-small) applies
especially well to marketplaces, where the temptation to build everything is
strong.

## Build in marketplace SEO from the start

For many marketplaces, organic search is the growth engine — and marketplace SEO
is different from a normal brochure site. Your value lives in many listing and
category pages, often location-based, each of which should be discoverable. That
means structured, indexable listing pages, sensible URLs, and content that scales
as your inventory grows. We built these foundations into Rent a Parking's city
and listing pages so the platform could attract organic traffic. It is far
cheaper to design this in than to retrofit it.

## Underneath, it's a real application

A marketplace looks like a website but behaves like a
[web application](/web-application-development): user roles, live data, search,
maps and structured listings all have real logic and edge cases. That is not
something a generic template can fake, which is why marketplaces are firmly in
[custom software](/marketplace-development) territory rather than page-builder
territory.
