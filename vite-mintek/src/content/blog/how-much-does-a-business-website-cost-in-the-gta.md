"How much does a website cost?" is one of the first questions every business
owner asks, and one of the hardest to answer in a single number. The honest
answer is that it depends on what the site needs to do. But "it depends" is not
helpful when you are trying to budget, so this guide breaks down what actually
drives the price and what you can expect in the Greater Toronto Area.

## A realistic starting point

At Mintek, business websites start from **CAD $1,500**. That figure covers a
professional, fast, mobile-friendly marketing site with the pages most small
businesses need: a strong homepage, services, an about section, and a contact
form that actually delivers enquiries. Where a project lands above that starting
point depends on scope, which is what the rest of this guide is about.

We do not publish an hourly rate, because hours are a poor proxy for value. What
matters is the outcome: a site that loads quickly, ranks, and turns visitors
into conversations.

## Typical price ranges

Every project is different, but most GTA small-business websites fall into a few
recognisable budget bands. Use these as a planning guide, not a fixed menu, the
right number depends on the scope factors covered below.

| Budget band | Typically a good fit for |
| --- | --- |
| **$1,500 – $3,000** | A focused, mobile-first marketing site: homepage, services, about, and a working contact form. Ideal for a new or small local business getting online properly. |
| **$3,000 – $6,000** | More pages and a more tailored design, richer content, a blog, and stronger on-page SEO. A credible presence for an established business. |
| **$6,000 – $10,000** | Custom design plus real functionality: bookings, integrations, location or menu pages, or content your team manages itself. |
| **$10,000+** | Sites that behave like applications, dashboards, logins, online ordering, marketplaces, or heavy integrations. At this point you are usually scoping a [web application](/web-application-development), not a marketing site. |

These are budgeting ballparks, not quotes. The rest of this guide explains what
moves a project from one band to the next.

## What drives the price

A website's cost is mostly a function of how much is custom and how much has to
be built rather than configured. The biggest factors:

### Number and complexity of pages

A five-page site is quicker to design and build than a twenty-page site with
distinct templates for services, locations, and case studies. More templates
means more design and more testing.

### Custom design vs. a template

A tailored design that reflects your brand takes longer than adapting a theme.
Custom is usually worth it for businesses that want to stand out, but a
well-executed template can be the right call when budget is tight.

### Functionality beyond content

Contact forms, booking, online ordering, payments, gated content, or a customer
login all add scope. Each one is a small system with its own edge cases. If your
"website" is really starting to behave like an application, that is a different
conversation, and often a
[web application](/web-application-development) rather than a marketing site.

### Content and SEO

Who is writing the copy? Do you need on-page SEO, structured data, and a sitemap
so search engines can find you? Fast, technically sound, SEO-ready sites take
more care than a page thrown together over a weekend, and that care is what makes
the site actually work for you.

### Integrations

Connecting your site to a CRM, email marketing, analytics, or a spreadsheet-based
workflow adds work. It is usually worth it, because it removes manual copying
later, but it is scope you should plan for.

## How to budget without guessing

You do not need to specify every detail before asking for a quote. A short,
honest description of what you are trying to achieve is enough to get a realistic
estimate. Focus on:

- **The goal.** More leads? Online bookings? Looking credible to larger clients?
- **The pages you know you need**, even roughly.
- **Any functionality** beyond publishing content (forms, payments, logins).
- **Who supplies the content**, and whether you need help writing it.
- **Your rough budget range**, so the proposal fits reality.

With that, a studio can scope a fixed first version rather than leaving you with
an open-ended bill. We prefer to quote clearly defined stages so you always know
what you are paying for.

## Cheaper isn't always cheaper

A very low quote often hides costs that show up later: slow load times, no SEO
foundation, a design you cannot easily update, or a form that quietly stops
working. Rebuilding a site a year in is more expensive than doing it once,
properly. The goal is not the lowest price; it is the best return on a sensible
budget.
