Hiring someone to build your software or website is a big decision, and the
market is full of options — from freelancers to large agencies. The hard part is
that if you are not technical, it is difficult to judge who is actually good. The
solution is not to learn to code; it is to ask the right questions. Here are the
ones that reveal the most, and the red flags to watch for.

## Ask who owns the code

This is the single most important question, and it is astonishing how often it is
skipped. **Once the project is complete and paid for, do you own the software and
its source code?**

The answer should be an unambiguous yes. With a custom build, the work is yours —
you should receive the code and deployment details so another developer could
maintain it if ever needed. If a provider is evasive here, or the arrangement
leaves you unable to move without them, you risk being locked into a platform that
holds your business hostage. We treat code ownership as standard on every
[custom software](/custom-software-development) project.

## Ask how they scope and price

Price matters, but *how* someone prices tells you more than the number. Good
questions:

- **How do you decide what to build first?** You want to hear about tying scope
  to a clear outcome and starting with a focused first version — the approach we
  describe in our guide on
  [scoping a software project](/blog/how-to-scope-a-custom-software-project).
- **Will I get a fixed quote for a defined scope?** A defined first version should
  come with a fixed price, not an open-ended meter.
- **What happens if scope changes mid-project?** There should be a sensible
  process, not silence followed by a surprise bill.

Be wary of two extremes: a big number quoted instantly before anyone understands
your problem, and a refusal to commit to any scope or price at all.

## Ask how you'll see progress

Projects that go silent between kick-off and launch are the ones that end in
tears. Ask: **how often will I see working software?** The healthy answer involves
building in stages with regular demos, so you can react early and cheaply rather
than discovering problems at the end. If the plan is "we'll disappear for three
months and come back with it," that is a risk.

## Ask what happens after launch

Software is not finished at launch — integrations change, needs evolve, and small
issues surface. Ask:

- **Do you offer ongoing support and maintenance?**
- **What happens if a connected service changes and breaks something?**
- **Can I have you make changes later, or hand it to my own team?**

You do not necessarily need a big ongoing contract, but you should understand your
options before you are stuck.

## Ask about honesty and fit

Some of the most valuable answers come from questions with no "right" spec:

- **When would you tell me *not* to build something?** A trustworthy developer
  will happily talk you out of an expensive build you do not need, or point you to
  off-the-shelf software when that is the smarter call.
- **Have you built something similar?** Relevant examples matter more than a long
  generic portfolio. Ask to see real work.
- **Will we meet in person?** For many GTA businesses, being able to sit down for
  discovery and handover is genuinely useful.

## Red flags to watch for

A few signals that should give you pause:

| Red flag | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Vague or evasive about code ownership | You could get locked in |
| A big fixed price before understanding the problem | It is a guess, and usually padded |
| No clear process or demos | High risk of a silent, runaway project |
| "Yes" to absolutely everything | Nobody honest agrees to every request |
| Only ever the cheapest option | Cheap rebuilds later cost more than doing it once |

Cheapest is rarely cheapest. A very low quote often hides costs that show up
later — no support, no ownership, or a fragile build you have to redo.

## The bottom line

You do not need technical expertise to hire well. You need to ask about ownership,
scope, process, support and honesty — and to trust clear answers over the lowest
number. A developer who explains their thinking in plain English is usually one
worth working with.
