"How much does automation cost?" is the practical follow-up to "should we
automate this?" The honest answer is that it depends on what you are connecting
and how messy the workflow is today. But "it depends" does not help when you are
trying to budget. This guide gives realistic starting points, the bands most
small projects fall into, and how to tell whether the spend is likely to pay for
itself.

## A realistic starting point

At Mintek, small, clearly defined [business automation](/business-automation)
projects start from **CAD $3,000**. That covers a focused workflow: for example
pulling data on a schedule, generating a recurring report, syncing two tools, or
sending reminders when something changes. Where a project lands above that
depends on how many systems are involved, how reliable their connections are, and
how much edge-case handling the process needs.

We do not publish an hourly rate. Hours are a poor proxy for value. What matters
is hours handed back to your team and errors that stop happening, priced as a
fixed scope wherever possible.

Even smaller ideas can suit a paid discovery or proof of concept: a low-risk way
to confirm the payback before you commit to a full build. The same starting point
applies to [spreadsheet automation](/spreadsheet-automation) when the work still
lives in Sheets and the tools around them.

## Typical budget bands

Use these as planning ranges, not a fixed menu. The right number depends on the
scope factors below.

| Budget band | Typically a good fit for |
| --- | --- |
| **Under $3,000** | A paid discovery or small proof of concept to validate one idea before a larger build. |
| **$3,000 – $7,500** | A clearly defined automation: one painful recurring task, a reliable connection between a couple of tools, or automated reporting from data you already have. |
| **$7,500 – $15,000** | Several related workflows, more integrations, validation and monitoring, or dashboards so the team can see what the automation is doing. |
| **$15,000 – $30,000** | Broader workflow automation across multiple systems, richer error handling, and more operational complexity. |
| **$30,000+** | Larger systems-integration work that starts to look like [custom software](/custom-software-development) or a [web application](/web-application-development), not a single background job. |

These bands match how we ask clients to think about budget on automation
projects. They are budgeting ballparks; a short discovery turns them into a
fixed quote for a first version.

## What drives the price

Automation cost is mostly a function of how many moving parts must cooperate
reliably.

### Number of systems and handoffs

Connecting two tools with a clean API is simpler than bridging three systems,
email, and a spreadsheet that people still edit by hand. Every extra handoff
adds mapping, testing and failure cases.

### How clean the rules are

Stable, rule-based steps are cheaper to automate than processes that change
every week or need human judgement mid-flow. If you cannot write the steps down
clearly, discovery time goes up before build time does.

### Data quality and edge cases

Missing fields, duplicate rows, renamed columns and "sometimes we do it
differently" exceptions are where automations get expensive. Cleaning and
validation are part of the scope, not an afterthought.

### Notifications, documents and dashboards

Quiet background sync is one thing. Generating documents, emailing stakeholders,
or giving managers a live view of status adds interface and testing work. Worth
it when people need visibility; optional when the job only needs to run.

### Monitoring and handover

A brittle script that only one person understands is not a finished automation.
Reliable projects include basic monitoring, clear ownership, and a short guide so
your team knows what happens when something fails.

## How to judge whether it is worth it

Price alone is the wrong question. Compare the build to the cost of *not*
automating:

1. **Hours per week or month** spent on the task today.
2. **Error and rework cost** when someone forgets a step or pastes the wrong
   figure.
3. **How often the process runs** and how stable the rules are.

If the work is repetitive, rule-based and error-prone, automation usually earns
its keep. If it is rare or constantly changing, the build may cost more than it
saves, and we will say so. Our guide on
[what business process automation is (and when it is worth it)](/blog/what-is-business-process-automation)
covers that decision in more depth, and
[five spreadsheet tasks worth automating](/blog/automate-repetitive-tasks-with-google-sheets)
shows concrete starting points many teams already recognise.

## Automation vs custom software vs a website

A quick orientation if you are comparing budgets across project types:

- **Websites** start from **CAD $1,500** and are mostly about being found and
  converting enquiries. See
  [business website costs in the GTA](/blog/how-much-does-a-business-website-cost-in-the-gta).
- **Automation** starts from **CAD $3,000** and quietly runs a process in the
  background.
- **Custom software** generally starts from **CAD $5,000** when people need to
  log in, manage data, or run a full workflow inside a purpose-built tool. See
  [custom software cost](/blog/how-much-does-custom-software-cost).

If staff are compensating for a tool with copy-paste every day, start with
automation. If they need a shared system with roles and a real interface, you are
usually scoping software, not a script.

## How to budget without guessing

You do not need a full technical specification to get a useful estimate. A short
description is enough:

- The task that hurts most, and roughly how long it takes.
- The tools involved today (spreadsheets, email, CRM, payments, and so on).
- What "done" looks like (report ready every Monday, no manual retyping, a
  reminder that never gets forgotten).
- Your rough budget band, so the proposal fits reality.
