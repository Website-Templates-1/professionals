Most articles about online ordering deal in generalities. This one does not: it
walks through a real project we built for **Airport Sweets and Tandoori**, a
Brampton restaurant, in 2022. It is a useful illustration of when a custom build
is worth it, what one actually contains, and how to read the results honestly.

## The problem: paying rent on every order

Like many independent restaurants, Airport Sweets and Tandoori relied on
third-party ordering. That gets you started, but it comes with a quiet, permanent
cost: a commission on every single order, plus little control over the customer
relationship. The order data, the contact details, and the repeat business
largely belonged to the platform, not the restaurant.

On thin restaurant margins, that adds up. The question was not "is online
ordering worth it?" — it clearly was — but "who should own the flow, the data,
and the margin?" When takeout is a real revenue stream, handing a slice of every
sale to a platform starts to look like rent you could stop paying.

## The solution: a system the restaurant owns

We built a fully [custom software](/custom-software-development) solution rather
than bolting features onto a template. The ordering flow, the payments, and the
customer data all belonged to the restaurant. The build included:

- **Real-time online ordering** with a cart, order tracking and payments.
- **Secure Stripe payments**, including promo-code handling for offers.
- **A real-time admin dashboard** for managing the menu, orders and offers.
- **Customer-behaviour analytics** derived from call history.
- **SMS retargeting** campaigns aimed at the restaurant's most frequent callers.

That last pair is the part most owners overlook. The system did not just take
orders; it turned the restaurant's own call history into a marketing engine,
automatically nudging the customers most likely to order again. Marketing that a
busy owner would never find time to do by hand ran quietly in the background.

## The results (from the project's own reporting)

Here is where honesty matters. The figures below come from the project's own
reporting for its peak month, not from a general industry benchmark, and they
describe one restaurant in one period. We share them because they are real, not
because they are a guarantee.

| Metric | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Customer calls in the peak month | **684** (up from an average of 523) |
| Return on investment in that month | **up to 705%**, depending on profit-per-order scenario |
| Customers who placed at least one order | **323** (18 of them ordered three or more times) |
| Orders during promotional periods | **173 of 290** |

The pattern underneath the headline numbers is the interesting bit: most of the
lift came from turning *existing* customers into repeat, direct orders, and a
large share of orders landed during promotions the system helped run. In other
words, the value was less about a flashy storefront and more about owning the
data and acting on it.

## What this case study actually teaches

A few lessons generalise well beyond one restaurant:

### Custom pays off when "almost fits" is costing you margin

Third-party apps are cheaper to start with, but they charge a percentage forever.
When order volume is high enough, a one-time build you own can beat a permanent
commission. This is the same "almost fits" tax we describe in our guide on
[custom software vs off-the-shelf](/blog/custom-software-vs-off-the-shelf) — except
here the tax was margin, not wasted hours.

### The best feature is often invisible

The SMS retargeting was not the headline, but it did work no one had time to do
manually. Good [custom software](/custom-software-development) frequently earns
its keep in the quiet background steps, not the obvious front-end.

### Numbers are only useful if they are honest

We frame these results as the project's own reporting for a specific month, and
we would not promise the same for a different restaurant. Any provider who
guarantees a percentage before understanding your business is guessing. For more
on how online ordering options compare, see our guide on
[how online ordering systems work for independent restaurants](/blog/restaurant-online-ordering-systems).

## Is it right for you?

A custom ordering system is a bigger investment than a link-out button, and it is
not the right first move for every restaurant. It makes the most sense when
takeout is core to your business and per-order commissions are visibly eating
your margin. If you are earlier than that, presenting your menu well and linking
to a platform you already use is often the smarter start.
