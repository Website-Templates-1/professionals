On **87** homepages Mintek could fetch for the 2026 Brampton study, **72 (82.8%)** contained the word “Brampton” in the page text. **15** did not. That is about one-sixth of the fetched pages.

The businesses were already associated with Brampton before anyone opened the homepage. The sample came from Google Places searches for categories in Brampton, and a listing had to show Brampton in its formatted address to stay in the set. A missing word on the site is not a finding that the business is somewhere else. It is a finding that this homepage’s text did not say the city.

This is a text check from **11 September 2026**, written up for [The State of Brampton Business Websites: 2026](/research/brampton-business-websites-2026). It is not a ranking study.

## What was measured

The check looks for the whole word “Brampton,” in any capitalization, in the homepage document after scripts, styles, and HTML tags are stripped away. The title text counts, because it sits between tags. The words a visitor can read in the body count. A meta description does not, because that sentence lives in an attribute the stripper throws away. Alt text on an image does not. A city name painted into a photograph does not.

“Peel,” “the GTA,” “Mississauga,” and “Caledon” do not count as a Brampton mention. A page can say it serves the region and still miss this particular signal.

The match does not know why the word is there. A footer address, a sentence about the service area, and a business name that includes Brampton all pass the same test. A blog excerpt that names the city in passing passes it too. The study did not score which of those was the “real” location line.

It also did not open the Google Business Profile. It did not compare the homepage with the map listing, the hours, or the categories on that profile. It did not check whether the business actually serves Brampton, only whether the fetched homepage text contained the word. Six URLs in the wider sample of **93** never returned HTML. They are outside the **87**, not inside the one-sixth.

## The neighbouring signals

Two other homepage patterns sit beside the city word, and neither one is a ranking.

Dedicated service-section links were detected on **42 of 87 (48.3%)**. The detector looks for an `href` containing a path such as `/services`, `/service`, `/treatments`, `/menu`, `/practice-areas`, `/what-we-do`, or `/our-work`. One matching link is enough. It does not open that URL, and it does not judge whether the destination has its own copy. A homepage that lists every service in one long scroll, with no such link, is a miss. A homepage that links to a thin `/services` page is a hit.

LocalBusiness- or Organization-style JSON-LD appeared on **36 of 87 (41.4%)**. That is a type-family match, not a count of valid Google LocalBusiness markup. The schema article, [LocalBusiness Schema: How Many Brampton Websites Actually Use It?](/blog/localbusiness-schema-brampton-websites), is where that distinction is spelled out. A page can name Brampton in a sentence and still have no JSON-LD. A page can have the JSON-LD and still bury the city in a field a person never reads.

Hours language is looser still. In a prior cut, an hours-like pattern matched **52 of 89** homepages (**58.4%**). The published study page shows **51 of 87 (58.6%)**. The pattern looks for words such as “Monday” or “hours,” or for opening hours inside schema. It is not a check that the hours are correct, current, or complete. It is a prior-cut neighbour to the city count, not a second finding about Brampton.

## Why the homepage should name the place, when that is true

A person who searched for a dentist, a restaurant, or a trades company in Brampton is trying to answer a plain question: is this the local one? The homepage is a reasonable place to answer it, in a sentence, when the answer is accurate.

That can be the street and the city in the footer. It can be one line about the area the business actually travels: Brampton, and the parts of Peel or the GTA it really covers. It should match the business. A shop with a counter on a Brampton street can say so. A crew that works across the region can say the region, and name Brampton if Brampton is part of that work. A page should not grow a city it does not serve so the word will match a search.

The study cannot tell us that the **72** homepages which included the word were clearer to a customer. It can tell us the word was available in the text. Clarity is a writing choice on top of that.

## NAP is a practice, not a guarantee

Name, address, and phone — NAP — are the same facts written the same way wherever they appear: the homepage, the Google Business Profile, a directory. The point of keeping them consistent is that a customer, and any program reading those pages, is looking at one business rather than three slightly different ones.

This study did not measure that consistency. It did not compare the homepage string with the Places listing, and it did not crawl directories. Address-like patterns and phone numbers exist as separate homepage checks in the study file. They are not a NAP audit, and they are not in the 82.8%.

Keeping the details identical is still worth doing when the details are true. It is a housekeeping practice. It is not, on this evidence, a lever that moves a ranking. [Local SEO for GTA service businesses](/local-seo-gta) is where that wider practice is described. Nothing in the Brampton count upgrades it into a promise.

## A service page and a long homepage are different pages

About half the fetched homepages had no detected service-section link. That can be a real gap, and it can be a homepage that already does the job in one screen.

A restaurant may only need the menu, the phone, and the address. Stuffing that into five URLs named after Brampton neighbourhoods does not make it more local. A firm with several distinct services can put each one on its own page so a visitor can open the one they need, with the city named where it belongs, once. The failure mode is the opposite of the long homepage: a set of near-copies that swap the city and keep the same paragraph.

The study’s 48.3% does not sort those cases. It only records the link. Choosing between one honest homepage and a few real service pages is a writing decision. It is not something the word “Brampton” settles.

## A missing word is a missing signal

The study’s rule for every homepage signal, including this one: absence was recorded as absence. It was not coded as “bad SEO.”

Fifteen fetched homepages did not contain the word. Some of those businesses may say Brampton on a contact page, in the map listing, or in a photograph of the storefront. Some may rely on “GTA” and consider that enough. The instrument did not grade those choices. A homepage can rank, or convert, or both, without this match. A homepage can include the word in a footer and still be slow, vague, or hard to call.

Treating the miss as a defect would invent a score the file does not have. The published study page says the same thing in the local-signal section: absence of an item is not automatically bad SEO.

## HTTPS and the viewport are already the usual case

These two are hygiene, not the story.

**85 of 87 (97.7%)** fetched homepages used HTTPS. A locked address bar was the normal result in this sample.

A viewport meta tag was uncommon as a miss. The report’s **4.5%** is **4 of 89** in a prior cut. The published page shows **3 of 87 (3.4%)** without one. Either figure is a small set of pages a phone may lay out at desktop width. Worth fixing on those pages. Not a description of how Brampton homepages handle local copy.

## How we'd write a local homepage without stuffing keywords

If the business is actually in Brampton, or actually works there, we would say that once in the copy a customer reads. The footer address can carry the street and the city. One sentence can carry the service area, limited to the places that are true. We would not put “Brampton” in every heading, every image name, and every service title to chase the 82.8%.

We would make the next fact the service, not another city. What the business does, who it is for, and how to start. If that work splits cleanly, it gets its own page, with the location repeated only where a new visitor needs it. If it does not split, it stays on the homepage. We would not manufacture a page per neighbouring city.

We would then check that the name, the address, and the phone on that page are the same ones on the Google Business Profile. Same words, same number. We would not add a second address to widen the map. The schema, if we add it, would repeat those same facts and nothing extra. That markup is the subject of the [LocalBusiness article](/blog/localbusiness-schema-brampton-websites), and it does not rank a site by existing.

We would not treat a missing “Brampton” as an SEO emergency, and we would not treat a present “Brampton” as a finished local page. The word is a signal that the text said the city. The page still has to be the right page for the business.

That belongs in a [web design in Brampton](/web-design-brampton) build as ordinary copy, beside the speed and the contact path. It is not a keyword package.

## What this study can and cannot tell us

It can tell us that on one day, **72 of 87** fetched homepages included the word “Brampton” in stripped page text, and **15** did not. It can tell us that a service-section link matched on **42**, and that a LocalBusiness- or Organization-style type matched on **36**. It can tell us HTTPS was already typical.

It cannot tell us who ranks for anything. It cannot tell us that the Google Business Profile is complete. It cannot tell us that a business which omitted the word does not serve Brampton — the sample was built from listings that already carried the city. It cannot tell us that adding the word changes traffic.

## Conclusion

Most of the fetched homepages did say Brampton. **72 of 87 (82.8%)** had the word in the text. About one-sixth did not. That split is a text signal on a set of businesses already tied to the city in Google Places. It is not a ranking, not a profile score, and not a judgment that the missing pages have bad SEO. The study recorded the absence and stopped there.

Service-section links and LocalBusiness-style markup were less common than the city word, and they are separate checks. Hours language at **58.4%** belongs to a prior cut (**52 of 89**); the published page’s hours figure is **51 of 87 (58.6%)**, from a loose pattern. HTTPS at **97.7%** is the usual case, not the lesson.
